**Psychedelic therapy is now legal in Australia. It is also, for most people, completely unaffordable.**

Since July 2023, authorised psychiatrists have been able to legally prescribe psilocybin for treatment-resistant depression and MDMA for PTSD. Australia became the first country in the world to do this. It was a genuine milestone.

But three years on, the average cost of a full three-dose MDMA treatment course runs at approximately $30,000 AUD out of pocket. Psilocybin therapy sits between $10,000 and $25,000 depending on the provider, the number of sessions, and the city.

Medicare does not cover it. Private health insurance does not cover it. Most Australians cannot afford it.

This is the conversation the field is not having loudly enough.

## Why Does Psychedelic Therapy Cost So Much?

The cost is not arbitrary. It reflects the actual resource intensity of the treatment model.

A typical psilocybin session involves a minimum of two therapists present for the entire dosing experience, which runs six to eight hours. Add preparatory sessions before the dose and integration sessions after it, a prescribing psychiatrist at every stage, facility costs, and pharmaceutical-grade psilocybin sourced through regulated channels, and the economics become clearer.

MDMA therapy is even more intensive. Three dosing sessions each requiring full therapist coverage, plus the surrounding preparation and integration work, can involve 50 to 80 hours of professional time per patient.

When you price that labour honestly, $30,000 is not a profit grab. It is what the model actually costs to deliver safely.

The problem is that the people who need it most are rarely the people who can afford $30,000.

## Who Is Actually Accessing It?

In the first year of Australia's program, the majority of patients accessing psilocybin and MDMA therapy were privately funded, educated, and living in metropolitan areas. Sydney, Melbourne, and Brisbane account for the vast majority of authorised prescriber locations.

Regional and rural Australians face a double barrier: cost and geography. Indigenous communities, who carry a disproportionate burden of the trauma and treatment-resistant conditions the therapy is designed to address, have been largely absent from the patient cohort.

This is the gap that makes Australia's psychedelic program genuinely impressive in its regulatory courage and genuinely troubling in its current delivery.

## The 5 Brutal Truths About Access in 2026

**Truth 1: Medicare will not cover it anytime soon.**

For Medicare to cover a treatment, it must be listed on the Medicare Benefits Schedule. That requires the therapy to go through a formal health technology assessment process. No application for psychedelic-assisted therapy has been lodged with the Medical Services Advisory Committee as of October 2026. This is a multi-year process even if one were submitted tomorrow.

**Truth 2: Private health insurance is watching but not moving.**

A Medibank Private study involving patients accessing psychedelic therapy through Australia's clinical registry is underway in 2026. This is the most important development in the access conversation. If Medibank Private data shows positive outcomes and cost offsets over time, it creates a commercial case for reimbursement. But that data is years away from informing a policy position.

**Truth 3: There are only a few hundred authorised prescribers in the country.**

As of mid-2026, fewer than 200 psychiatrists have registered as authorised prescribers for psilocybin and MDMA combined. The therapist workforce required to support them remains a primary bottleneck. Some estimates suggest that treating 1,000 patients per year in Australia would require more than 100 authorised prescribers alongside a therapist workforce in the low thousands. The infrastructure does not yet exist at that scale.

**Truth 4: The model that gets results is the expensive one.**

There is a real tension in the field between preserving the therapeutic container and scaling access. The outcomes data that has driven the regulatory approval of psilocybin and MDMA therapy comes almost entirely from the expensive, intensive model: long sessions, two therapists, robust preparation and integration. Stripping that model back to reduce costs risks stripping out the mechanism of action. The field does not yet know how to make this cheaper without making it less effective.

**Truth 5: The underground is already solving the access problem, imperfectly.**

The reality is that thousands of Australians are not waiting for the regulated system to become affordable. They are accessing psilocybin and MDMA outside clinical settings, often through retreat networks, trusted facilitators, or self-directed practice. The risk profile of underground access is meaningfully different from clinical access. But for someone who cannot afford $30,000 and has been treatment-resistant for a decade, it is not a difficult calculation.

The Psychedelic Digest does not advise or endorse accessing psychedelics outside the legal framework. But pretending this population does not exist would be dishonest journalism.

## What Could Actually Change

Three developments have genuine potential to shift the access equation within the next three to five years.

Private health insurance reimbursement is the highest-leverage single change available. If one major insurer moved to cover psychedelic therapy for specific indications, the market economics would shift quickly.

Group therapy models are being explored by several providers. Delivering the integration and preparation work in a group format, while maintaining one-on-one dosing, could reduce per-patient cost by 30 to 40 percent without fundamentally compromising the therapeutic model.

Nurse practitioner prescribing rights would dramatically expand the prescriber workforce if the regulatory framework evolved to include them. This is being discussed but has not yet been formalised.

## What You Can Do Right Now

If you are living with treatment-resistant depression or PTSD and cannot afford private treatment, there are some pathways worth knowing about.

Clinical trials remain the most accessible route to legal psychedelic therapy in Australia. Participants typically receive the treatment at no cost. Trials recruiting in 2026 include studies at Monash University, University of Melbourne, and UNSW. Use the [Psychedelic Digest clinical trials page](https://www.thepsychedelicdigest.com/trials) to find what is currently recruiting near you.

Some providers operate sliding scale fees for eligible patients. This is not widely advertised but is worth asking about directly.

Harm reduction resources, including the [Alcohol and Drug Foundation](https://adf.org.au), provide state-specific information for people who are navigating this outside clinical settings.
