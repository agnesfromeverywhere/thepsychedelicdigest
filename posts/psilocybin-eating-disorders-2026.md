Anorexia nervosa has the highest mortality rate of any psychiatric condition. It kills more people than schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, or major depression. And the treatment success rate has barely improved in decades. Against that backdrop, the early data on psilocybin for eating disorders is not just interesting. It is urgent.

## Why Are Existing Treatments Failing?

Anorexia nervosa and other serious eating disorders are not primarily about food. They involve profoundly rigid cognitive patterns, severe self-criticism, distorted perception of the body and self-worth, and often a deep ambivalence about recovery.

**Standard cognitive-behavioural therapy, nutritional rehabilitation, and hospitalisation have response rates of around 50% for anorexia**, with high relapse rates even among those who initially recover. For treatment-resistant cases, which represent a large proportion of people with chronic anorexia, there is effectively no approved pharmacological treatment. The neurobiology of severe anorexia appears to be highly resistant to change through existing means.

This is exactly the profile that psychedelic therapy has shown the most promise for: rigid, treatment-resistant conditions characterised by inflexible neural patterns and impaired self-compassion.

## What Did the Imperial College London Study Find?

The first formal study of psilocybin for anorexia nervosa was conducted at Imperial College London and published in [The Lancet eClinicalMedicine](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanpsy/article/PIIS2215-0366(22)00184-5/fulltext) in 2023.

Ten women with longstanding anorexia nervosa received two doses of psilocybin alongside psychological support. The results were more promising than researchers expected.

**All ten participants showed measurable reductions in eating disorder symptoms.** Five showed clinically significant improvements by established criteria. Anxiety and depression scores decreased substantially. Participants reported increased self-compassion, reduced rigidity in thinking about food and body, and a greater sense of connection to themselves and others.

At the three-month follow-up, improvements were largely maintained. No serious adverse events occurred. The study was small and uncontrolled, but the signal was clear enough to justify larger trials.

## What Is Happening at the University of Melbourne?

The University of Melbourne is currently running one of the few active psilocybin trials for eating disorders in the world. It is the most significant Australian contribution to this specific research question and is being watched closely by eating disorder researchers globally.

The trial is examining psilocybin-assisted therapy in adults with anorexia nervosa who have not responded adequately to standard treatment. Participant recruitment is ongoing. Results are expected in 2027.

## Why Might Psilocybin Work for Eating Disorders?

The theoretical mechanisms align well with what we know about the neurobiology of eating disorders.

**Increased neuroplasticity.** Psilocybin has been shown to rapidly increase dendritic spine density and neural connectivity in the prefrontal cortex, a region heavily implicated in cognitive flexibility and self-regulation. Anorexia is characterised by extreme cognitive inflexibility.

**Reduced self-criticism and increased self-compassion.** Multiple studies have found that psilocybin reduces activity in brain networks associated with self-referential negative processing. Participants consistently report feeling less harsh toward themselves. This is directly relevant to eating disorders, where merciless self-criticism is often central.

**Ego dissolution.** The experience of temporarily losing the sense of a fixed, defended self, which psilocybin can produce at higher doses, appears to loosen entrenched identity structures. For people whose identity is deeply organised around restriction and control, this dissolution can be genuinely therapeutic.

**Processing underlying trauma.** A significant proportion of people with eating disorders have histories of trauma. Psilocybin's demonstrated efficacy for trauma processing may address a root cause rather than just the symptomatic behaviour.

## What Are the Safety Considerations?

People with eating disorders often have medical complications including electrolyte imbalances, cardiovascular changes, and compromised liver function that require careful medical evaluation before any psychedelic therapy.

**Psilocybin therapy for eating disorders should only be pursued in a clinical setting with comprehensive medical monitoring.** The Imperial College London study included thorough physical health screening before each dosing session precisely because of these risks.

This is not a condition where self-medication or informal ceremonial use of psychedelics is appropriate. The medical complexity requires clinical infrastructure.

## FAQ

**Is psilocybin approved for eating disorders in Australia?**
No. The TGA Authorised Prescriber scheme currently covers psilocybin only for treatment-resistant depression. Eating disorders are not an approved indication. Access for people with eating disorders is currently only available through clinical trials.

**What eating disorders are being studied with psilocybin?**
Anorexia nervosa has received the most research attention. Some researchers are also studying psilocybin's potential for bulimia nervosa and binge eating disorder, but these are at earlier stages of investigation.

**How do I find a psilocybin eating disorder trial in Australia?**
Check [ANZCTR](https://www.anzctr.org.au/) and [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/) for active studies. The University of Melbourne trial is the most relevant Australian study currently active.

**Is psilocybin safe for people who are medically compromised due to their eating disorder?**
This requires individual clinical assessment. People with significant medical complications from eating disorders need thorough evaluation before any psychedelic therapy. The decision must be made by a qualified clinical team with full knowledge of the person's medical status.

**What are the alternatives if I cannot access a trial?**
Integration therapy, ketamine therapy (which has broader prescribing access), and standard eating disorder treatment remain available.
